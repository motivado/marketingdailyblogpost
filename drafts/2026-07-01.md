<p>Robots can now write essays, solve math problems, and even compose music. So here is the question every parent and teacher is quietly asking: <em>what should our children be learning right now?</em> The surprising answer is not more screen time or more rote memorisation — it is creativity. Not as an art class add-on, but as the core skill that defines confident, capable thinkers who can thrive no matter how fast the world changes.</p>

<h2>What Children Build When They Create</h2>
<p>When children create — whether they are designing a marble run, building a cardboard robot, or figuring out why their circuit does not light up — they develop something far more powerful than a finished product. They develop the habit of trying, failing, adjusting, and trying again. This iterative loop is the foundation of creative confidence. Research consistently shows that children who engage in open-ended, hands-on making activities build stronger problem-solving skills, more flexible thinking, and greater emotional resilience. Creativity is not a talent you are born with — it is a practice you build, one project at a time.</p>

<h2>Why Hands-On Making Matters More Than Ever</h2>
<p>In an AI-driven world, machines will keep getting better at executing known tasks. What they cannot replicate is the human spark — the moment a child looks at a pile of materials and says, <em>"What if I build this differently?"</em> Hands-on learning puts children in that exact moment again and again. When a child physically builds something, they engage their spatial reasoning, their sequencing logic, and their imagination all at once. The act of making creates a kind of deep learning that no worksheet or video tutorial can match. And crucially, it teaches children that their ideas have value — a belief that stays with them long after the project is finished.</p>

<h2>Simple Ways to Nurture Creativity at Home and in the Classroom</h2>
<p>You do not need expensive equipment to get started. Give children open-ended challenges rather than step-by-step instructions: <em>"Can you build something that moves?"</em> goes much further than <em>"Follow these steps."</em> Celebrate the process, not just the result — the failed prototype that led to the breakthrough matters more than the polished final product. At home, set aside time for unstructured making: cardboard, tape, recycled materials, and curiosity are the only supplies needed. In the classroom, swap one "right answer" activity per week for an open design challenge, and watch how differently your students engage, collaborate, and surprise you.</p>
