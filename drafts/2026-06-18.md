### Title
Why Creativity Is the Skill Every Child Needs in an AI World

### Body

Ask any parent what skill they most want their child to master, and you'll likely hear answers like coding, mathematics, or English. But ask the engineers at the world's leading AI companies, and they'll give you a surprising answer: **creativity**. In an age where machines can write code, analyse data, and pass exams, the one thing they still cannot do is genuinely imagine something new.

**What creativity actually builds in children**

Creativity is far more than drawing pictures or making music. In children, it is the engine behind divergent thinking — the ability to look at a problem from multiple angles and generate solutions that didn't exist before. When a child builds a robot from scratch, designs a contraption that solves a classroom problem, or figures out why their first attempt failed, they are exercising the same cognitive muscles that will make them valuable, adaptable thinkers for decades to come.

Research consistently shows that children who engage in open-ended, hands-on challenges develop stronger problem-solving skills, higher emotional resilience, and deeper confidence in their own ability to figure things out. Unlike memorising facts — a skill AI now surpasses — **creative thinking grows stronger every time it is challenged**.

**Why hands-on learning unlocks creative confidence**

There is a meaningful difference between watching a creative process and being inside one. When children work with physical materials — wiring a circuit, testing a mechanism, redesigning something that broke — they learn that mistakes are data, not failures. This "making" mindset is the foundation of creative confidence: the belief that you can try, fail, adjust, and try again.

For parents and teachers looking to nurture this, the most powerful thing you can do is give children open-ended challenges with no single right answer. Instead of "build this model following these steps," try "build something that moves using only these three materials." The messier the process, the richer the learning. Celebrate what they discovered, not just what they built.

**Creativity in the classroom: tips for educators**

- **Replace instructions with provocations.** Pose a problem, offer materials, and step back. Let children struggle productively.
- **Make iteration visible.** Display first drafts and final versions side by side. Show that improvement is the goal, not perfection.
- **Ask "what if" questions.** "What if your robot had to carry something heavy?" opens up more thinking than "Did you finish?"
- **Protect free-build time.** Even fifteen minutes of unstructured making per week builds creative habit.
