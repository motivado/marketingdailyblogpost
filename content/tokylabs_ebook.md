	### 1.1 Why traditional classroom models are evolving to embrace STEM, and robotics in particular.

	Education is undergoing a profound transformation, shifting from traditional models to more dynamic, student-centric approaches. The increasing integration of robotics into classrooms is a direct response to this change. Unlike purely theoretical disciplines, robotics remains highly resilient to the impact of AI. Its emphasis on practical application, problem-solving, and hands-on experimentation makes it less susceptible to automation, ensuring that students develop skills that remain relevant in the workforce.
	A major shift in modern education is the move toward student-centric learning, where students take an active role in their education rather than passively absorbing information. One of the most effective methodologies for this transition is the flipped classroom model. In this approach, students learn theoretical concepts at home through books, videos, podcasts, or AI-powered resources. Then, during class time, they engage in hands-on experiences, applying their knowledge through experiments, projects, and collaborative problem-solving exercises. Since Robotics is inherently practical, this methodology fits seamlessly, creating an environment where assessments happen through action rather than traditional exams. This not only enhances learning but also reduces the concern that students might rely on AI tools to complete their assignments, as their understanding is demonstrated through real-world applications.

	Here is an example of this socratic dialogue in practice:
	At Tokylabs, in late 2024 we were hired to co-manage a **hackathon**, an event where participants collaborate intensively to develop innovative solutions to a given problem. Our role included defining the central topic, and we chose **plastic pollution** as the challenge to tackle. Rather than jumping to quick solutions, we encouraged all teams to ask themselves **“why?”** at least three times, uncovering the deeper causes of the issue and possibly inspiring them to advocate for meaningful change.
	To start, we presented a **case study** on plastic waste floating in the ocean, highlighting activist groups that use boats to collect plastic still lingering on the surface. When participants asked **“why”** this waste accumulates in the ocean, they realized that much of it originates from beaches, where plastic debris is eventually carried away by tides. This led them to explore companies focusing on beach cleanups. Asking **“why”** again, they traced the problem back to rivers, where another major portion of plastic waste originates before flowing into the sea. NGOs are actively working to intercept plastic at this stage. Finally, questioning **“why”** once more revealed that plastic pollution in rivers is largely due to improper disposal, often involving **single-use plastics**. This exercise helped participants see that while ocean cleanup is crucial, addressing pollution upstream—before plastic reaches waterways—could be even more impactful.
	But why focus on the rivers if organisations like **Bye Bye Plastics** are dedicated to education, raising awareness, and creating political pressure to reduce plastic consumption in the first place? This final **“why”** pushes us to reflect on the systemic nature of the problem. While cleanup efforts in the ocean, beaches, and rivers are crucial, they are reactive solutions—addressing the consequences rather than the root cause. True impact comes from **preventing plastic waste at its source**, which is precisely what advocacy groups aim to achieve through education, policy change, and shifting consumer behavior.
	This **iterative questioning process** allowed hackathon participants to move beyond surface-level solutions and explore deeper, systemic interventions. Whether tackling the issue through cleanup, prevention, or policy, the exercise demonstrated that the key to solving complex problems lies in understanding their **underlying causes**—a skill essential for both robotics education and real-world problem-solving.

	**3.2 Benefits of Screen-Free Learning for Primary School Students**
	While digital tools play an important role in modern education, excessive screen time can have detrimental effects on young learners. Research has indicated that prolonged screen exposure is linked to issues such as reduced focus and potential vision problems \[5\]. In contrast, screen-free, hands-on learning experiences offer significant advantages:
	- **Tactile Engagement:** Physical interaction with materials—using puzzle-like blocks and kid-friendly components—promotes deeper understanding and enhances psychomotor skills. This type of engagement is especially beneficial for children aged 6 to 9 years, who thrive on play-based learning \[7, 16\].
	- **Enhanced Cognitive and Emotional Development:** Without digital distractions, students can focus more intently on tasks, leading to improved concentration and emotional regulation.
	- **Alignment with Market Trends:** Global market analyses have shown a significant shift toward tactile, screen-free learning tools, emphasizing a move away from purely digital education models \[1, 2\].
	- **Maintaining Traditional Learning Values:** Despite the rise of digital platforms, traditional classroom tools remain relevant and effective, as documented in studies on educational technology trends \[6\].
	In addition to that, hands-on, screen-free projects allow children to explore robotics in a way that promotes open-ended inquiry and creative problem-solving. As students build and assemble their projects, they are encouraged to experiment and iterate, learning valuable lessons in trial and error. This process helps them develop several key skills:
	- **Confidence:** By working playfully with materials that are easy to shape, mold, and fix—often avoiding the rigidity of hard plastics or metal—children gain confidence in their ability to create and modify their projects.
	- **Dexterity and Craftsmanship:** The act of manipulating physical components enhances fine motor skills and dexterity. This hands-on craftsmanship is critical for developing the hand–eye coordination necessary for later, more complex tasks.
	- **Psychomotor Skills:** Building simple projects requires students to coordinate their mental planning with physical execution. This process strengthens their psychomotor abilities, laying a foundation for more advanced technical skills.
	- **Creativity:** Autonomous building encourages creativity. As students construct something unique from scratch, they not only exercise their imaginative faculties but also learn to innovate and adapt their ideas in real time.

	### **7.2 Tools and Curriculum to have age and level differentiation in the same classroom**

	We have adopted and customised a very smart system from  **LOFI ROBOT (17) ** (huge thanks to Maciej!) that allows us to use the same projects in the classroom while easily adjusting them for all skill levels—without adding extra work for the teacher.

	**Early Years of Primary School**
	Young primary school students may struggle with tasks like measuring, drawing precise dimensions, and building based on a 2D blueprint. It’s also important to consider the tools they feel comfortable using for assembling, cutting, and gluing. For safety reasons, tools like hot glue guns and cutters should be avoided. Instead, materials and tools should be adapted to their needs. For example, scissors should have larger handles to fit multiple fingers and shorter blades to create more leverage, making cutting easier.
	Here are three key guidelines to follow:
	- They can use a printed template to cut out shapes with adapted scissors instead of drawing the design directly onto the cardboard. Cutters should not be used.
	- Screwdrivers are used just to poke holes.
	- Instead of nuts and bolts, they should use glue sticks and Blu Tack to attach pieces together.

	Here is the step-by-step process we suggest for the early years **(photos courtesy from LOFI ROBOT). **

	1. **Print the design in a A4 Sheet**

	2. **Prepare cardboard sheet and cover it with paper glue**

	3. **Pay attention to cover whole surface of cardboard with paper glue**

	4. **Glue printed cardboard robot template to the cardboard sheet**

	5. **Smooth out the surface of the glued robot template**

	6. **Pre-cut all the parts from the sheet**

	7. **Precisely cut out all the robot parts along solid lines**

	8. **Use scissors to trace bending lines along dashed lines**

	9. **Use the screwdriver to make holes in the corners of the object to cut it out**

	10. **Use scissors along with ruler to trace holes for servo motors on both sides of the cardboard**

	11. **Gently remove inner element from cut out hole**

	**Late Years of Primary School**
	Older primary school students have better dexterity for designing, drawing, and measuring, as well as a stronger ability to visualize how to build from a 2D blueprint. They can also handle more complex tools, such as screwdrivers, nuts, and bolts.
	To adapt activities to their skill level, we follow these two main guidelines:
	- Students receive a **2D printed blueprint** with measurements and must **draw the design directly onto the cardboard** using pens, rulers, etc. They can then cut the parts with larger scissors or, if age-appropriate, a cutter.
	- Whenever possible, **students should avoid glue** and instead **use nuts and bolts** to join pieces. This approach provides a more authentic experience with mechanics and robotics.

## About the author:

	<columns>
		<column>
			Eduardo Alarcón is the Founder and CEO of [TokyLabs](http://tokylabs.com/), the Edtech company who created an innovative STEM Robotics tool and methodology for total beginners that it's changing the way students and teachers approach technological innovation. Eduardo is also Maker, Stakeholder of XinChejian (First Hackerspace in China), TEDx Speaker and former Participant of NASA Space Elevator beam power challenge.
			With over a decade of experience in robotics education, Eduardo learned the hard way—making countless mistakes along the journey. Determined to bridge the gaps in learning, he founded his own robotics company, gaining firsthand insight into the real challenges faced by beginners.
			After working with institutions, educators, and makers worldwide, he realized that accessible, streamlined courses were essential to overcoming learning barriers and achieving results faster. Now, through his training programs, he distills years of experience into practical, hands-on learning, accelerating the careers of aspiring robotics professionals across the globe.

			He also holds six years of experience as researcher at Universitat Politecnica de Catalonia in Spain, where he designed and implemented a multi-model power system for researching on Smart microgrids. Before moving to Shanghai, Eduardo designed Smart Bank, a fin-tech management platform for banks and financial institutions capable of categorizing and visualizing user transactions as well as making recommendations. He has seven publications in some of the top international scientific magazines to his name on topics ranging from static synchronous compensators to the NASA space elevator. He speaks five languages and has travelled to more than 45 countries.

			His quote:
			> “*Motivation is a conscious mental state”*
